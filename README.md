# Artificial Intelligence Course

**Instructor:** Srinivasa Sai Chava · Boston University

Course materials for the Artificial Intelligence & Data Science programme — slides, teaching
notebooks and practice sets, published session by session.

**Covered so far: Day 1 – Day 33** (Modules 1 & 2 complete · Module 3: Machine Learning in progress)

*Day numbering is this repo's own running sequence; the bracketed topic references (2.1, 2.4, …) are the ones from the course syllabus.*

---

## How the sessions run

| | |
|---|---|
| **Format** | 90 minutes concepts and live coding, then 30 minutes hands-on practice |
| **Environment** | Google Colab — nothing to install |

Each session ships with four things:

1. **Slides** (`.pptx` and `.pdf`) — what we work through together in class
2. **Teaching notebook** — every example from the slides, runnable, with extra detail
3. **Practice questions** — the 30-minute hands-on set
4. **Practice solutions** — worked answers with explanations, **posted after the session**

### Day 24 — The Normal Distribution

*Topic 2.4. Where the standard deviation stops describing and becomes a ruler.*

**The curve** — two numbers define it entirely: μ for where, σ for how wide · why it recurs

⚠️ **When it does not apply** — on real salary data the 2σ check passes at 95.0% against a
predicted 95.4% **by pure coincidence**, while every other check fails badly. One passing

### Day 25 — Binomial & Poisson

*Topics 2.5–2.6. Days 23 and 24 measured quantities. Today we count events — and counts need
their own distributions.*

**What makes a situation binomial** — four conditions: fixed n · two outcomes · the same p
every time · independent trials. Three examples that qualify and three that fail, each
breaking a **different** condition

**The formula, built rather than memorised** — all 8 outcomes of three coin flips written
out, giving 1 · 3 · 3 · 1 → that is `C(n,k)` → one path is `p³(1−p)²` and every path with the
same count gives the same product → `C(n,k) × pᵏ × (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ`, worked through by hand for
7 heads in 10 flips

**Using it** — `pmf` for *exactly*, `cdf` for *at most*, and ⚠️ **`1 − cdf(k−1)` for
*at least***. The off-by-one that gives 0.0755 instead of 0.2642 — a factor of 3.5, with no
error message. Mean `np`, sd `√(np(1−p))`

**Poisson** — for a rate with no countable n: calls per hour, typos per page. One number, λ
— and **λ must match your interval**. A call centre averaging 3 an hour is overwhelmed
**one hour in five**, which no average could ever tell you

⭐ **mean = variance = λ** — a free diagnostic. If `var/mean` is well above 1 the events are
clustering and Poisson is the wrong model

**Choosing** — *"out of"* → binomial, *"per"* → Poisson. Then: binomial **becomes** Poisson
as n grows and p shrinks, and both become Day 24's bell curve

### Day 26 — The Uniform Distribution

*Topic 2.6 (continued). The simplest distribution there is, and the one every other one is built from.*

**Discrete uniform** — n outcomes, each 1/n. Mean (a+b)/2, variance (n²−1)/12 · and the
three-library bounds trap: `scipy` and `numpy` exclude the top value, Python's own `random`
includes it, and none of them errors when you get it wrong

⭐ **Probability becomes AREA** — the idea that separates continuous from discrete. A range has
an area and therefore a probability; a single point has zero width, so **P(X = any exact
value) = 0** — which is not the same as impossible, because no instrument ever measured
anything to infinite precision

⚠️ **A density is not a probability** — U(0, 0.1) has a height of **10**. Density is
probability *per unit of x*, like a price per kilo, and `pdf()` never returns a probability

⚠️ **The scipy trap** — `uniform` takes loc and **width**, not two endpoints. `U(a,b)` is
`uniform(a, b−a)`. Get it wrong and you get a silently wrong upper bound

**Is it really uniform?** — same range and same mean do **not** make it uniform; only the shape
does · `chisquare` for discrete, `kstest` for continuous · and a die that lands on 6 twice as
often as it should is caught by 60 rolls only **40%** of the time

**Benford's law** — leading digits of real data are nowhere near uniform; 1 appears **2.7×**
as often as "random" would give. It is how auditors spot invented numbers

**The foundation** — every random number your computer makes is U(0,1), reshaped. Inverse
transform sampling builds a normal, an exponential and a loaded die from one flat line

**One uniform is flat. Twelve added together are a bell curve** — the third unexplained
appearance of the normal in three sessions

### Day 27 — The Central Limit Theorem

*Topic 2.10. Three sessions have ended with an unexplained bell curve. This is the explanation,
and the result the rest of the module rests on.*

⭐ **The one distinction** — your **data** and your **sample mean** are two different
distributions, and almost every mistake in this topic comes from confusing them. The theorem is
about the mean. ⚠️ It says nothing about your data, which stays exactly as skewed as it always
was — a million salaries are no more normal than a hundred

**The theorem** — sample means are normal, centred on μ, with sd **σ/√n**, from *any*
population with a finite σ. Demonstrated on an exponential, a bimodal, a uniform and a lumpy
three-valued population — including an honest look at the one where n = 30 is visibly not
enough

**σ vs the standard error** — σ describes people and never shrinks; SE describes the average and
shrinks as √n. **4× the data buys 2× the precision**, which is a budget, not a detail

⚠️ **"n = 30 is enough" is simply not true** — measured coverage of a "95%" interval at n = 30:
normal 94%, uniform 94%, exponential 91%, **lognormal 87%**. And lognormal is what income,
spend, file size and session length all look like. Even n = 500 only reaches 94%

**The rule that actually works** — skew of the mean = population skew / √n, *exactly*. So
**n ≈ (skew / 0.3)²** — with the honest caveat that a skew estimate from a small sample is both
noisy and biased low

⚠️ **Two ways it fails** — **infinite variance** (a Cauchy mean has the same spread at n = 1 and
n = 10,000; collecting more data buys nothing) and **dependence** (100 correlated readings were
worth 11 independent ones, making every interval 4× too narrow)

**What it buys you** — a worked confidence interval on average customer spend, where a normality
test on the data fails at p = 4e-18 and the interval is correct anyway · what "95% confident"
does and does not mean · and an honest measurement that n = 200 gives 92% coverage, not 95%

The practice notebook ends by building a bootstrap and finding it barely helps — which is the
intended lesson.

### Day 28 — Hypothesis Testing

*Topics 2.11–2.13. The maths is small — it is yesterday's standard error with a decision rule bolted
on. The misuse is enormous, and that is what the session is actually about.*

**The logic** — assume nothing is happening, then measure how surprising the data is.
`t = (what we saw − what we expected) / SE`. A full worked paired t-test, reported the way it
should be: effect, interval, effect size, exact p

**What a p-value is** — under the null it is **uniform**, which is why 5% of nothing looks like
something. ⚠️ And the four misreadings: it is *not* the chance the null is true, *not* the
chance you are right, *not* evidence of no effect, and *not* a measure of importance

⚠️ **p = 0.049 vs p = 0.051** — a difference of **0.017** in the test statistic separates a
discovery from a file drawer

⚠️ **scipy's default two-sample test is the wrong one** — `equal_var=True` fires **44%** of the
time on data with no effect when the smaller group has the larger variance. The fix is one
argument

⚠️ **Paired data** — the same example goes from **0%** detection to **98%** by using
`ttest_rel`. The largest single gain from a design choice in the whole module

**Power** — 80% needs n = 25, 63 or 393 per group depending on the effect size. ⚠️ And "not
significant" ≠ "no effect": a wide interval around zero means you learned nothing, a narrow one
means you learned something real

⚠️ **How it goes wrong** — 20 tests → **64%** chance of a false positive · peeking as data
arrives → **16%** · adding four subgroups → **20%** · and the base rate: if 5% of your
hypotheses are true, **fewer than half** your "significant" findings are

**What to report instead** — the effect size, a confidence interval, the test and n, the exact
p, and **how many tests you ran**. The last one is the only one that takes courage.

The practice notebook asks you to p-hack a null dataset on purpose, then write the honest
version of the same report.

**What r measures** — how tightly a cloud hugs a **straight** line, and nothing else.
⚠️ **r is not a slope:** four datasets with fitted slopes from **0.09 to 90** all returned
**r = 0.697**. And `r²` is the number that keeps you honest — r = 0.3 is **9%** of the
variation

⭐ **Anscombe's quartet** — four datasets with the same mean, the same sd, the same regression
line and **r = 0.816**, and four completely different pictures. The habit that follows is
non-negotiable: **plot it**

⚠️ **r only sees straight lines** — a perfect U, a wave, an X and a ring, every one of them a
near-deterministic relationship, all returning **r under 0.07**. Dose–response curves live
here. **Spearman** fixes monotonic curves and outliers; it does *not* fix a U-shape

⚠️ **One point** — a single typo turned **r = −0.14 into r = +0.64** with p = 4.7e-13, and a
genuine **r = 0.96 into −0.20**. Spearman barely moved in either case, which makes the gap
between the two coefficients the cheapest outlier detector there is

**How well do you know r?** — r = 0.50 from **n = 10** has a 95% interval of **−0.19 to
+0.86**: it contains zero *and* almost perfect. ⚠️ At the other end, n = 100,000 makes
**r = 0.006** significant, explaining **0.004%** of the variance

⚠️ **The five explanations** — chance · **confounding** (ice cream and drownings, r = 0.82,
falling to 0.05 once temperature is held still) · **reverse causation** · **selection**, where
two genuinely independent variables correlated at **−0.73** among the people who were chosen ·
and only then, cause

⚠️ **Simpson's paradox** — women admitted at a higher rate in **every** department and a lower
rate **overall** (34.3% vs 55.0%), from one table with no arithmetic error in it

⚠️ **`df.corr()` on 20 columns is 190 hypothesis tests.** On pure noise, seven came back
p < 0.05 and the strongest reached r = 0.45, p = 0.003. The Bonferroni threshold is 0.00026
and nothing survived it, correctly

The session ends with a six-question checklist, three questions of which cannot be answered
from the data at all. The practice notebook asks you to manufacture a finding out of nothing,
then diagnose which of the five explanations a real-looking correlation actually is.

### Day 30 — A/B Test Design & Analysis

*Topic 2.19. The last statistics session, and the only one in the whole module where you are
allowed to say "caused". Everything from Day 23 onwards gets used here at once.*

⭐ **Randomisation is the entire trick** — the same users give a **+21** effect
observationally and **+2.4** when a coin decides the arm (true effect: +2.0). A coin flip
switches off **confounding, reverse causation and selection** simultaneously, and leaves only
chance — which is what the rest of the module taught you to handle

**Three decisions before launch** — the **unit** (the user, never the pageview), **one**
primary metric, and the size

**Sizing** — `n_per_arm(p0, mde)` in one line, cross-checked against statsmodels.
Baseline 10%, catching **+1pp** → **14,751 per arm**, 7.4 days. ⚠️ Halve the effect you want
to catch and the cost **roughly quadruples**: +0.5pp needs 57,763 per arm and a month

⚠️ **The winner's curse** — with a true lift of +1.00pp, an 11%-power test reports **+3.32pp**
among the tests that "win", with a significant p-value attached. A properly powered test
overstates by only 1.12×. **Power is not just about finding effects, it is about measuring
them**

⚠️ **Peeking** — checking the dashboard 20 times before the planned end turns a 5%
false-positive rate into **25%**, on data with no effect in it

⚠️ **Sample ratio mismatch** — a 51.5/48.5 split on 40,000 users is **p = 2e-09**. That is a
bug, not bad luck, and it invalidates the whole test. One line of chi-square, and it is the
highest-value check in the process

⚠️ **Novelty and weekends** — day 1 reads **+1.79pp**; by day 14 the daily lift is **+0.02pp**
and the cumulative figure still reads +0.45pp. The baseline itself swings **2.1pp** between
weekdays and weekends. Run whole weeks, and plot the daily lift, not just the cumulative one

⚠️ **Revenue metrics** — the common claim that heavy tails break the t-test is wrong: the
false-positive rate measured **3–4%**, slightly conservative. The real damage is to power. A
**real +10% lift** has **9%** power at n = 1,000 (the same lift on a well-behaved metric: 96%).
Capping the top 1% in advance takes power at n = 20,000 from 56% to **94%**

⚠️ **Segments** — slicing a flat test eight ways gives a **34%** chance of a fake winner

**Deciding** — three non-significant tests, and only one of them tells you anything. The
decision comes from where the **interval** sits relative to a **ship threshold**, never from
the p-value. "Not significant" ≠ "no effect"

A full worked example end to end — plan, SRM check, analysis, secondary metric, decision, and
the eighty-word report that contains everything a reader needs.

### Day 31 — What Machine Learning Is

*Topics 3.1 + 3.2. The first machine learning session, rebuilt from zero. Nothing is
assumed — no earlier maths, no earlier Python. One idea per slide, a picture on every one,
and every claim measured rather than stated.*

**You already use this, several times a day** — the deck opens with six things students
already rely on (spam filtering, YouTube suggestions, face unlock, Maps, fraud alerts,
keyboard predictions) and the point that nobody wrote the rules for any of them

⭐ **Try to write a spam filter by hand, and watch it fail** — one rule tested on six real
emails, with a tick or a cross on each row. **4 right out of 6** — and both failures are
honest messages sent to the spam folder, which is the worst mistake you can make

⭐ **The flip, in one diagram** — ordinary programming takes rules + data and produces
answers; machine learning takes data + answers and produces the rules. Same three things,
arrows reversed. That is the entire definition

**The vocabulary on a real table** — sample, feature, label, model, annotated directly on
four laptops with their prices

⭐ **What "learning" actually is** — five phones, their RAM and their price. Three attempts
at a model with the error bars drawn each time: **254 → 14 → 1.2**. No mystery, just two
numbers being nudged until the error shrinks

⚠️ **The most important rule, measured** — a decision tree scored a **perfect 1.000** on
the rows it studied and **0.644** on rows it had never seen. A straight line scored 0.925
and 0.840. One memorised, one learned, and only hiding rows revealed the difference

**The three families** — supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement, told apart by one
question: *do we already have the answers?* Then regression vs classification, a decision
chart for naming any problem, and six real problems worked through

⚠️ **Why a model cannot read "Dell"** — a live `ValueError`, and the reason: every model
multiplies its inputs, and there is no answer to "Dell × 0.4"

⚠️ **Numbering categories invents an order that is not there** — Apple=0 … Lenovo=4 tells
the model that Lenovo is four times Apple and that HP sits halfway between Dell and
Lenovo. Measured on **600 laptops**: the average error **tripled, from ₹3,340 to ₹10,240**,
and the score fell from 0.960 to 0.642. Same data, same model — only the encoding changed

**One-hot encoding, built step by step** — one yes/no column per category, exactly one 1
per row, and no category bigger than any other

**When numbering IS correct** — Small/Medium/Large has a real order, and on 500 coffee
orders numbering ties one-hot exactly (₹5.25 either way) using **one column instead of
three**. The test is one sentence: *is one of these genuinely MORE than another?*

⚠️ **When one-hot goes wrong** — 900 delivery rows with 426 different pincodes becomes
**426 new columns**. The model stops learning and starts memorising: **+0.561** on studied
rows, **−0.494** on new ones, which is worse than guessing

### Day 32 — Getting Data Into Shape

*Topics 3.3 + 3.4, rebuilt in the Day 31 style. Before a model can learn anything, the
columns have to be in a shape it can read. One idea per slide, a picture on every one, and
every number on this page was measured, not estimated.*

⭐ **Watch a model pick the wrong phone** — you are holding an 8 GB phone at ₹30,000.
Phone A has the **same RAM** and costs ₹2,000 more. Phone B has **double the RAM** and
costs ₹500 more. The computer measures the distances as **2,000 and 500** and confidently
picks B. After scaling they become **0.23 and 1.83**, and it picks A. Nothing about the
phones changed — only the units

**The price column is not more important, it is just bigger** — a bar showing the price
column shouting **5,000 times louder** than the RAM column, which is the whole problem in
one picture

⚠️ **So who actually needs scaling?** Measured on 500 phones, three models side by side:

| Model | no scaling | with scaling |
|---|---|---|
| k-nearest neighbours | **0.056** | **0.872** |
| linear regression | 0.886 | 0.886 |
| decision tree | 0.825 | 0.825 |

Only the model that **measures distance** moved. The tree changed by exactly zero, because
"is the price above ₹20,000?" is the same question however you write the number

**The three scalers, worked by hand** — the formula and the arithmetic for `StandardScaler`,
`MinMaxScaler` and `RobustScaler`, each one on the same six numbers

⚠️ **What one outlier does to each** — five salaries of ₹30k–50k and one CEO on ₹400k, run
through all three and laid out as a table you read across. Min-max squeezes the five real
salaries into a spread of **0.05**; robust leaves them at **1.60** and simply reports the
CEO as sitting **28.6 IQRs out**. One value did that, and it did not even have to be wrong

⭐ **The column a model can never invent** — predicting the tip on a food order. A model
can **add** its columns and **multiply** them by weights, but it can never **divide** one
by another. So `bill ÷ items` is permanently out of reach. Handing it over moved the score
from **0.390 to 0.883** — no new data, nothing collected, two existing columns rearranged

⭐ **A date is never a feature** — the same timestamp used three ways on 900 deliveries:
the raw number scores **−0.007**, the hour scores **+0.232**, and "is it between 7pm and
10pm?" scores **+0.637**. Same column, three different amounts of thinking

**Four recipes you will use again and again** — divide one column by another · pull a date
apart · add things up · make a yes/no flag

⚠️ **More columns is not better** — 250 rows, 3 real columns, then junk piled on top. The
two lines go in opposite directions: the studied-rows score climbs **0.885 → 0.981** while
the new-rows score falls **0.854 → −0.454**, which is worse than guessing the average.
Every added column was pure random noise. Then `SelectKBest` finds the three real columns
again and the score comes back to **+0.854**

⚠️ **The most expensive mistake of the day** — 100 rows, 3,000 columns of random numbers
and a random target. Nothing can predict it, so the honest answer is zero. Pick the ten
best columns **before** splitting and you get **+0.421** — a discovery made of nothing. Put
the same picker **inside** the pipeline and it reports **−0.280**, the truth

**The two leaks, side by side** — a leaked **scaler** cost **0.0007**. A leaked **column
picker** cost **0.70**. It is the same keystroke; the difference is how many lottery
tickets the leaking step got to buy with your test rows

**The order that keeps you honest** — split, engineer, scale, select, train. With the one
piece of jargon worth knowing: `.fit()` *learns* the recipe, `.transform()` *applies* it,
and the hidden rows only ever get `.transform()`

The practice set ends by handing you a model that scores **0.998** with a correct split, a
correct scaler and no bug anywhere — and asks you to prove it is worth nothing.

### Day 33 — Drawing the Best Line

*Topic 3.5, rebuilt in the Day 31 style. Your first machine learning model. The whole
thing is done on FIVE students first, with numbers small enough to check in your head,
and only then on the computer. Every number on this page was measured, not estimated.*

⭐ **Five students, one question** — hours of study per week against exam mark. A new
student does 6 hours. What will she score? She is not in the table, so you need a **rule**

**A line is a rule you can carry around** — `mark = 8 × hours + 26` answers for students
who were never in your data. And notice what it does *not* contain: it does not store the
five students, it stores **two numbers**

⭐ **Measuring how wrong a line is** — the error on one student drawn large. Then the
first idea, and why it fails: a good line and the **exactly upside-down** line both have
errors that add to **zero**, because the pluses and minuses cancel. The score tells you
nothing

⭐ **So square them first** — the full table, row by row: errors 0, +2, −3, 0, +1, adding
to 0 but squaring to **14**. Now "best" has a definition you can compute, and the name
**least squares** stops being jargon

⭐ **The squares drawn as actual squares** — each error becomes a real square on the chart,
so you watch the total shaded area shrink from **104 down to 14** as the line improves.
That picture is where the name comes from

**Why squaring, and not just dropping the minus sign** — five misses of 2 and one miss of
10 are both "10 marks wrong" in total, but squaring says the single blunder is **five
times worse**. That is the judgement we want

⭐ **The bowl** — try every slope and plot the error: 174, 104, 54, 24, **14**, 24, 54,
104, 174. Perfectly symmetric, exactly one bottom, no other dips to get stuck in

⭐ **Route 1 · the formula, worked by hand** — the full table of `hours − 3`, `mark − 50`,
their product and `(hours − 3)²`, totalling **80** and **10**. So the slope is 80 ÷ 10 =
**8**, and the intercept follows because the best line always passes through the average
point (3 hours, 50 marks)

⭐ **Route 2 · gradient descent, one step by hand** — before any code. The two nudges work
out to **−332** and **−100**, so one step at a learning rate of 0.03 takes the slope from
0 to **9.96** and drops the error from **13,154 to 1,518**

⚠️ **And watch it run** — the slope *overshoots* to 13.79 before coming back to 8. The
honest lesson: watch the **error**, not the parameters. The error only ever goes down —
1518 → 530 → 415 → 309 → 72 → **14.0**

**Two routes, the same two numbers** — the formula in one calculation, gradient descent in
about 800 steps, both landing on slope 8 and intercept 26. Learn the second one properly:
the formula is a shortcut that only exists for straight lines, while walking downhill is
how essentially every modern model is trained

**Say the slope out loud** — *"one extra hour a week is worth about 8 more marks."* If you
cannot finish that sentence, you have not understood your own model

⚠️ **The intercept trap** — 26 is the line's answer at zero hours, but nobody in the data
studied fewer than 1. It is an extrapolation, and you should not quote it

⭐ **R², honestly** — **1 − (14 ÷ 654) = 0.979**, drawn as a picture: the flat line at the
average 50 against the fitted line, with the errors marked on both. R² compares your line
with guessing the average, and that is **all** it does

⚠️ **An R² of 0.75, and the line is still wrong** — a second dataset where studying helps
a lot at first and then levels off. The score looks respectable, but the errors swing
**−4.7, +5.0, +2.2, −3.5** across the range. A pattern in the errors beats any score

**Sixty students instead of five** — the slope drops from 8 to **5.44**, R² 0.896, average
miss 4.5 marks. Five students was never enough to pin the slope down, and that is the real
lesson

**Split first** — 0.900 on the rows it studied, 0.883 on rows it never saw. A gap of
**0.017**, against the Day 31 decision tree that scored a perfect 1.000 and then collapsed
to 0.644. A straight line has two numbers to adjust; it cannot memorise

⚠️ **Never extrapolate** — nobody studied more than 11.9 hours. Ask the line about 25 and
it answers **164 marks on a paper out of 100**, without blinking

**Five mistakes everybody makes once** — including the one that gets people into print:
*hours cause marks*. A line measures that two things move together, and is completely
silent about why.

The practice set ends by handing you a board memo with an **R² of 0.93** and asking you to
find the **two separate reasons** its conclusion does not follow.
